ED is often a health signal, not only a symptom

Erections depend on blood vessels, nerves, hormones and mood working together. A good evaluation treats the symptom and checks what it may be telling you.

The short answer

What is erectile dysfunction treatment?

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is a persistent difficulty getting or keeping an erection firm enough for satisfying sex. Treatment begins with an evaluation, because ED can be an early marker of cardiovascular disease, diabetes or hormone problems. From there, the first-line option for most men is an FDA-approved oral PDE-5 inhibitor such as sildenafil or tadalafil. Men who cannot take or do not respond to tablets may be offered injection therapy with FDA-approved alprostadil or compounded Bimix or Trimix, started with a supervised in-office test dose. We also offer low-intensity shockwave therapy and platelet-rich plasma (PRP); neither is FDA-approved for ED, and both are explained plainly below.

Concerns we evaluate

  • Difficulty getting or keeping an erection
  • Tablets that stopped working or never worked well
  • Side effects from a current ED medication
  • ED alongside low energy, low libido, weight gain or poor sleep
  • ED after prostate treatment or with diabetes

Regenerative options

Shockwave therapy and PRP

We offer both. They differ from the treatments above in one way you should know before choosing: neither is FDA-approved to treat ED, and the evidence is still developing. We would rather you hear that from us.

Low-intensity shockwave therapy

Low-energy acoustic waves are applied to the penis over a series of short sessions, with the aim of improving blood flow. No anesthesia is needed and there is no downtime.

Where the evidence stands: the FDA has not approved shockwave for ED. The AUA guideline considers it investigational. European urology guidelines give it a weak recommendation for men with mild blood-flow-related ED, men who prefer not to take or cannot take tablets, and men who respond poorly to them.

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP)

A small sample of your own blood is processed to concentrate platelets, which are then injected into the penis after numbing.

Where the evidence stands: PRP is not FDA-approved for ED. The AUA guideline considers it experimental, and European urology guidelines say current evidence is not yet enough to recommend it and that larger trials are needed. Some studies show modest improvement; results are not guaranteed.

Neither therapy replaces a medical evaluation, and we do not quote success rates. If one is appropriate, your provider explains what is known, what is not, and how it compares with FDA-approved options before you consent.

Candidacy

Who may—or may not—be a candidate

Who may be considered

Adult men with ongoing erection difficulties who complete a medical, sexual and psychosocial history and a focused examination, with selected lab testing. Cardiovascular fitness for sexual activity is considered first, because sex is physical exertion. Men whose tablets have not worked are often good candidates for a second look: the AUA guideline recommends titrating the dose and giving clear instructions before concluding a tablet has failed.

When treatment may not be appropriate

Oral PDE-5 inhibitors must not be used with any nitrate medicine (such as nitroglycerin) or with riociguat. Recent heart attack or stroke, unstable heart disease or very low blood pressure need cardiology input first. Injection therapy is contraindicated in men predisposed to priapism—such as sickle cell disease or trait, multiple myeloma or leukemia—in men with penile fibrosis or a penile implant, and needs extra care with blood thinners.

Informed choice

Important risks, limitations and alternatives

Risks & limitations

PDE-5 inhibitor labeling warns about low blood pressure with alpha-blockers or other blood-pressure medicines, prolonged erection, sudden vision loss (NAION) and sudden hearing loss; stop and seek care for any sudden change in vision or hearing. Injection therapy can cause bruising, pain and, with repeated use, penile scarring or curvature. With any treatment, an erection lasting more than 4 hours is priapism and needs emergency care. No treatment cures the underlying cause, and results vary.

Alternatives to discuss

  • Treating contributors: blood pressure, diabetes, weight, sleep apnea, smoking, alcohol and medication effects
  • Counseling or sex therapy, alone or with medication
  • Vacuum erection devices
  • Urology referral for intraurethral alprostadil or a penile implant
  • Low-intensity shockwave therapy or PRP, described below, which are not FDA-approved for ED
Medication transparency

How we use compounded medication

FDA-approved products come first. A compounded preparation is considered only when a prescriber determines that a specific difference is clinically necessary for you—for example a dosage form you can use, a strength that is not commercially available, or a combination injection such as Bimix or Trimix after an approved option has not worked.

Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved. FDA guidance is explicit that a lower price on its own does not justify compounding a copy of a commercially available drug, and that the prescriber must document the patient-specific reason.

What that means for you

  • You are told when a medicine is compounded
  • The clinical reason is recorded on your prescription
  • The dispensing pharmacy and its license status are verified
  • Compounded products are never described as FDA-approved or equivalent

Common questions

Erectile Dysfunction Treatment FAQ

Can erectile dysfunction be a sign of heart disease?
It can. The American Urological Association advises that men be counseled that ED is a risk marker for underlying cardiovascular disease and other health conditions. That is why treatment starts with a medical history, examination and selected lab testing rather than a prescription alone.
Can I take ED medication if I use nitroglycerin or another nitrate?
No. Sildenafil and tadalafil labeling states they are contraindicated with any form of organic nitrate, and with guanylate cyclase stimulators such as riociguat. The combination can cause a dangerous drop in blood pressure. Tell your provider about every medication you take, including ones used only occasionally.
What is the difference between alprostadil, Bimix and Trimix?
All three are injected into the side of the penis to produce an erection. Alprostadil is available as FDA-approved products such as CAVERJECT and EDEX. Bimix (papaverine and phentolamine) and Trimix (papaverine, phentolamine and alprostadil) are compounded preparations: they are not FDA-approved, and a prescriber chooses them only when a patient-specific clinical reason supports it.
Why does injection therapy start in the office?
The AUA guideline says an in-office injection test should be performed for men considering injection therapy, and alprostadil labeling directs that the first injections be given in the provider's office. The test visit sets a safe starting dose, teaches technique and checks for a prolonged erection before you inject at home.
Can I get ED treatment by telehealth?
Oral medication may be prescribed through a telehealth visit for patients physically located in Texas, North Carolina or South Carolina, after an evaluation and when clinically appropriate. Injection therapy begins with an in-office visit. Labs or an in-person examination may be required.
Does low testosterone cause ED?
Sometimes it contributes, but ED has many causes, including blood-vessel, nerve, medication, psychological and relationship factors. The AUA recommends measuring morning total testosterone in men with ED, and notes that for men with confirmed testosterone deficiency, oral ED medication may work better when the deficiency is also treated.
Do shockwave therapy and PRP work for ED?
Both are offered here, and both are still being studied. Neither is FDA-approved to treat ED. The AUA considers low-intensity shockwave investigational and PRP experimental. European urology guidelines give shockwave a weak recommendation for men with mild blood-flow-related ED, and say the evidence for PRP is not yet enough to recommend it. We explain this before you decide, and we do not promise results.
Is compounded ED medication FDA-approved?
No. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved and are not reviewed by the FDA for safety, effectiveness or quality. FDA guidance also states that a lower price on its own does not justify compounding a copy of a commercially available drug; the prescriber must identify a specific difference that matters for that patient.
