The short answer

What is microneedling?

A professional microneedling device uses multiple fine needles that repeatedly enter and leave the skin at controlled depths. The resulting microchannels trigger a wound-healing response that can gradually remodel collagen and improve selected textural concerns. It is not the same as a superficial home roller, a facial, laser resurfacing or RF microneedling.

Concerns it may address

  • Selected facial acne scars
  • Selected facial wrinkles and fine lines
  • Uneven-looking surface texture
  • Patients seeking gradual remodeling rather than an ablative procedure
  • Abdominal surgical scars only when the specific authorized device and plan support that use
Regulatory clarity

What FDA authorization does—and does not—mean

FDA has legally authorized a limited number of microneedling devices for specific uses, body areas and patient ages. Marketing authorization applies to the named device and indication, not every pen, roller, body area or combination.

Authorized-use examples

FDA identifies legally marketed uses that include improving the appearance of facial acne scars, facial wrinkles and abdominal scars in patients age 22 and older. The patient labeling for the exact device determines its authorized treatment area and intended use.

Important limitations

FDA has not authorized microneedling devices to deliver cosmetics, topical medications, vitamin solutions, drugs or blood products such as PRP into the skin. It also has not cleared microneedling devices for hair loss. Combining devices and products may have unreviewed risks.

Ask which device is being used. A reputable consultation should identify the device, its authorized use, whether a new sterile cartridge is opened for the session and why the proposed depth fits the treatment goal.
Candidacy

Who may—or may not—be a candidate

Who may be considered

Adults with selected acne scars, facial wrinkles or textural concerns may be considered after skin, scar and medical assessment. Skin tone alone does not automatically exclude treatment, but pigment-change risk, device evidence and conservative settings require individual planning.

When treatment may be inappropriate

Microneedling may be postponed or avoided for active infection, painful or pus-filled acne, active herpes, a fresh tan or sunburn, impaired healing, immune suppression, uncontrolled diabetes, bleeding disorders, anticoagulant therapy, current or recent isotretinoin, keloid history, active rash, certain autoimmune or skin diseases, pregnancy, breastfeeding, treatment-area cancer or radiation, or allergy to device materials or anesthetic. Final exclusions depend on the device labeling and medical evaluation.

Your visit

What happens during treatment

Evaluate & prepare

Your provider reviews the concern, skin type, scar pattern, medications, prior procedures and healing history. Skin is cleansed; photographs and topical anesthetic may be used.

Treat methodically

A new sterile needle cartridge is opened. Device depth and speed are selected by area, and controlled passes create an even treatment endpoint without unnecessary trauma.

Protect the barrier

The skin is soothed and protected. You receive product-specific instructions for cleansing, sun protection, makeup, exercise, heat and restarting active skincare.

Informed choice

Risks, limitations and alternatives

Common and less common risks

Dryness, roughness, tightness, redness, itching, peeling, discomfort, burning, bruising, pinpoint bleeding and crusting can occur. Less common risks include infection, cold-sore reactivation, swollen lymph nodes, persistent lines, scarring and darker or lighter pigment. Results may be incomplete, temporary or cosmetically unsatisfactory.

Alternatives to discuss

  • Medical skincare for pigment, acne or barrier concerns
  • Chemical peels for selected superficial texture or pigment
  • LaseMD or laser rejuvenation when energy-based treatment is appropriate
  • Dermaplaning for superficial exfoliation and vellus-hair removal—not scar remodeling
  • Dermatology or surgical scar evaluation when the concern exceeds a cosmetic treatment
Critical distinction

Conventional versus RF microneedling

Conventional microneedling

Fine needles create mechanical microchannels without intentionally delivering radiofrequency heat. Depth, cartridge sterility and treatment technique still matter, and risks remain.

RF microneedling

RF devices place microneedles into or beneath the skin and deliver heat through the needle tips. This changes the mechanism and risk profile. This page does not represent RF treatment as equivalent to conventional microneedling.

FDA safety communication: In October 2025, FDA reported serious complications with certain uses of RF microneedling, including burns, scarring, fat loss, disfigurement, nerve damage and cases requiring medical or surgical treatment. Patients should ask whether RF is proposed, which device will be used and what specific training the provider has.
Why experience matters

More depth is not automatically better

Safe microneedling depends on diagnosis, device selection, sterile technique, cartridge control, appropriate depth, treatment endpoint and aftercare. Aggressive passes can increase inflammation, pigment change and scarring without guaranteeing a better result.

Identity Aesthetics approach

  • Concern and scar pattern before device settings
  • Individualized depth by treatment area
  • New sterile cartridge for every session
  • Conservative planning for pigment-prone skin
  • Clear escalation for infection, scarring or unexpected healing
Access & pricing

In-office consultation and treatment

Microneedling requires an in-person skin examination and procedure. Telehealth can support general education or follow-up only when clinically appropriate; it cannot replace the treatment visit.

Pricing: Consultation or skin assessment is required. Cost varies by treatment area, device, session plan and medically appropriate add-ons. Always ask about current promotions and package savings.

Common questions

Microneedling FAQ

What is microneedling?
Microneedling uses a professional device with fine needles that repeatedly enter and leave the skin, creating controlled microchannels intended to initiate a wound-healing and remodeling response.
What concerns has FDA authorized devices to treat?
FDA has authorized a limited number of devices for specific uses including improving facial acne scars, facial wrinkles and abdominal scars in adults age 22 and older. Authorization is specific to the device, body area and labeling.
Is microneedling painful?
Topical numbing medication is commonly used. Patients may still feel pressure, vibration, warmth or pinpricks, particularly over bony areas or at greater treatment depths.
How much downtime should I plan?
Redness, tightness, dryness, sensitivity or mild swelling commonly last several days. Peeling, bruising or pigment changes can last longer. Your provider should give instructions based on the actual device and treatment depth.
How many sessions will I need?
Many concerns are treated as a series rather than a single session. The number and spacing depend on the scar or wrinkle pattern, device, depth, healing response and goals.
Can microneedling deliver PRP, vitamins or medication?
FDA states that it has not authorized microneedling devices to deliver cosmetics, topical medications, vitamins, drugs or blood products such as PRP into the skin. Combination safety and effectiveness may not have been reviewed.
Is RF microneedling the same treatment?
No. RF microneedling adds radiofrequency heat beneath the skin and has different risks. FDA issued a 2025 warning about reports of serious complications with certain RF-microneedling uses.
Can I use an at-home roller instead?
Home rollers are not equivalent to professional medical microneedling. If they puncture skin or are overused, they can contribute to irritation, infection, scarring or spread of warts and herpes.
Can microneedling be done over active acne?
Treatment is generally postponed over painful, pus-filled or deep active acne because needling can worsen inflammation and spread infection. Acne control may come first.
Is microneedling available by telehealth?
No. The examination and procedure are in-office. Selected follow-up questions may be handled remotely only when your provider determines that is appropriate.
